Matters of Life and Death

Year 11 opens. You take away your Grade 9 workbook and your Companion today — the Companion's two tasks per week come BEFORE the lesson, and that discipline starts now.

Lesson 1

The origins and value of the universe

Big question How do Christians respond to the Big Bang and the origins of the universe? · spec 1.4.1

What this is about

The Big Bang is not a guess — redshift, cosmic background radiation, gravitational waves and the Higgs boson all point back to it. The sharper question is what follows. Three Christian responses diverge: creationism holds Genesis is right and science wrong (with Apparent Age explaining an old-looking young universe); intelligent design argues the universe's irreducible complexity demands a designer; the compatibility response says Genesis teaches WHO created and WHY, science the HOW — and notes the theory's own author, Georges Lemaître, was a Catholic priest.

For the (d), do not treat the three as rivals only: all agree God is the ultimate cause, that creation is good, and that humans are stewards, not owners of a commodity. Pope Francis told the Pontifical Academy the Big Bang 'does not contradict the intervention of the divine creator, but requires it' — set that against the atheist claim that a self-starting universe leaves God with nothing to do, then weigh which survives.

Lesson 2

The origins and value of human life

Big question How do Christians respond to evolution? · spec 1.4.3

What this is about

Evolution by natural selection is the strongest-evidenced account of how life developed — and each Christian response pays a different price for its answer. New-age creationists reject it outright, reading fossils as remains of Noah's flood. Intelligent design allows change within species but says complexity demands a creator. The compatibility view, voiced by Pope Francis, holds that God 'created human beings and let them develop according to the internal laws that he gave to each one'.

The Grade 9 move is to see what hangs on Adam: touch that first domino and original sin, the Fall and the logic of salvation move with it — which is exactly why the debate matters beyond biology. Against all three stands Dawkins' claim that evolved life 'has no special value other than what humans choose to give to it'; answer it with Genesis 1:27, humans made in the image of God, and weigh whether value needs a giver.

Matters of Life and Death

Four mock topics down. Everything this week is examinable in November — and the summaries below are arguments to master, not stories to remember.

Lesson 1

Death and the afterlife

Big question Why do people believe in life after death? · spec 1.4.5

What this is about

Why believe in life after death at all? The Christian case starts from evidence, not wishful thinking: all four Gospels record the empty tomb, and Paul argues in 1 Corinthians 15 that as 'in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive'. Around it sit other cases: near-death experiences studied by Parnia and van Lommel, mediums, and reincarnation evidence such as remembered past lives — each weaker than it first looks, and the textbook says so.

Analyse the reasons in two families: evidence-based (resurrection, NDEs, the paranormal) and need-based (meaning, comfort for the bereaved, and justice — the demand that the good are finally rewarded). A top answer tests each on its own terms: an NDE proves an experience, not a destination; the justice argument proves a desire, not a fact. Then weigh whether the resurrection is history or faith — that is where the marks live.

Lesson 2

The natural world

Big question What are Christian and non-religious attitudes to animals and the natural world? · spec 1.4.8

What this is about

Every side in the environment debate quotes Genesis — the argument is over which verse governs. Dominion readings stress 'fill the earth and subdue it'; stewardship readings answer that a steward is a tenant, not an owner, and the landlord checks the state of the place. Christians split over animals too: some say only humans bear God's image, so research and food use are acceptable; others reply that not one sparrow 'will fall to the ground apart from the will of your Father'.

Non-religious positions run from conservationist Humanism to pure cost-benefit reasoning about finite resources and global warming. Note the deep agreement before you evaluate: the Assisi Declaration's line that dominion means 'to protect, not destroy' is common ground between the divergent views — so the strongest (d) answers argue about motive (obedience to God vs prudence for our grandchildren), not about whether to recycle.

We cross to Islam and Paper 2. Your Unit 8 Grade 9 workbook is issued today; the analytical register stays the same.

Lesson 1

Muslim attitudes towards peace

Big question Is Islam a religion of peace? · spec 2.4.1a

What this is about

Is Islam a religion of peace? Start with the word: Islam is linked to salaam, the greeting is 'salaam alaykum', and the Qur'an calls the faith Dar as Salaam, the House of Peace. Surah 2:208 commands believers to 'enter into peace wholeheartedly', and Surah 25:63 praises those who answer the ignorant with 'Peace'. Peace here is not passivity but a state of justice — which is why the tradition insists on putting wrongs right.

The divergence is real: some Muslims choose confrontation, and Islam debates whether they can claim the name of a faith whose meaning they contradict. The Grade 9 move is to refuse the false choice — quote the texts, acknowledge the minority, and argue that a religion is defined by its sources and mainstream practice, not by its most violent members. Then weigh whether that defence would satisfy a sceptic.

Lesson 2

Why wars occur

Big question Responses to the causes of war — should religious people fight? · spec 2.4.3

What this is about

Wars rise from nationalism and ethnicity, from resources, and sometimes from religion — but should religious people fight? Muslim teaching is double-edged: if a war is just (self-defence, ending injustice) a Muslim MUST fight, because the Qur'an commands it and Muhammad himself fought in self-defence; but a war of aggression is forbidden. Humanists split: many are pacifists who would strengthen the UN, while some atheists accept fighting for a just cause.

For evaluation, use Archbishop Fitzgerald's analysis: religion 'isn't usually the cause' of conflict but 'an added factor' — the real roots are social and political. That is the steelman-killer for 'religion causes wars'. Notice too how many traditions reach for Situation Ethics here, asking whether NOT fighting would be the greater injustice. Judge the underlying cause, not the banner.

Peace and Conflict

The Peace and Conflict mock topics are complete — half the mock is now taught. If a divergent view still blurs, this is the week to sharpen it.

Lesson 1

Pacifism

Big question Should religion and pacifism go together? · spec 2.4.4

What this is about

Pacifism is the refusal to fight — but its roots differ by tradition, and the differences are the exam. Christian pacifism starts at the Sermon on the Mount: no resistance, turn the other cheek; the early Church refused arms for three centuries and the peace Churches still do. Most Muslims reject that — jihad means struggle, and Muslims under attack are expected to fight back; there is no turning the other cheek in Islam — yet a genuine Muslim tradition of passive resistance to injustice exists.

Humanist pacifism runs from Erasmus through Kant and Bentham to CND: war as waste, judged by reason not revelation. The subtle point that earns the ninth grade: pacifism in Islam and Judaism is a minority tradition, not an absent one — sweeping claims fail. Weigh whether pacifism is the highest form of faith or, as its critics say, a luxury paid for by those willing to fight.

Lesson 2

The victims of war and the work of religious organisations

Big question Who helps the victims of war, and how? · spec 2.4.2a & 2.4.3

What this is about

War's victims are counted in casualties (over 60 million dead in the Second World War), displacement (millions of refugees and internally displaced people today) and expense. Who helps, and how? The Ahmadiyya — 'Love for all, hatred for none' — run peace forums and denounce terrorism; the Muslim Peace Fellowship practises a non-violent 'prophetic jihad'. Humanists helped found the United Nations and campaign to strengthen it; Christian, Jewish and Muslim agencies bandage, feed and repatriate.

The analytical edge: real peace work does both jobs — it bandages the wounded AND drains the swamp that wounded them, which is why inter-religious dialogue matters (Fitzgerald calls common ground between faiths 'crucial for world peace'). Surah 41:34 supplies the theology: 'repel evil with what is better', and your enemy becomes 'a friend and intimate'. Evaluate which contribution — relief or reconciliation — peace ultimately depends on.

Back to Christianity. The mock will ask about these two topics directly; know the named sources, not just the positions.

Lesson 1

Families

Big question What is the purpose of the family? · spec 1.2.3

What this is about

What is the family FOR? Christian teaching answers: it is God's basic unit of society, the place where children are raised safely, taught moral values, and brought up 'in the training and instruction of the Lord' (Ephesians 6:4). Notice the covenant is mutual, not one-way: children obey, parents nurture — and Ephesians attaches a promise, 'that it may go well with you'. Some Christians dissent on one point: the family should encourage children toward faith, not manufacture it.

The secular account keeps every function except the religious one: safety, moral formation, emotional and financial support — 80% of parents expect to go on helping their adult children. So the divergence is narrow but deep: not WHETHER the family matters, but whether its purpose includes handing on faith. A sharp (d) exploits that narrowness — agreement on nurture, disagreement on nurture's goal — and weighs whether the religious purpose adds anything the secular family lacks.

Lesson 2

Family planning

Big question What are the different attitudes to contraception? · spec 1.2.5

What this is about

The contraception debate is not about whether to plan a family — even Catholic teaching commends 'responsible parenthood' — but about the METHOD. Humanae Vitae reasons that sex has a unitive and a creative purpose which must not be split: artificial contraception separates what God joined, so only natural family planning is permitted. Non-Catholic Christians reply that the Bible nowhere forbids contraception, that God made sex to cement marriage as well as to make children, and that all methods are permissible to LIMIT a family rather than refuse one.

Secular reasoning judges by consequences: contraception prevents unwanted pregnancies and STDs, transforms women's lives, and makes 'every child a wanted child'. For the top marks, name the mechanism in dispute — some contraceptives act as abortifacients, which imports the abortion argument — and weigh whether the unitive/creative distinction is profound theology or a distinction without a difference.

Marriage and the Family

All new mock teaching ends this week. From here to the paper it is retrieval, argument practice and the (d).

Lesson 1

Divorce and remarriage

Big question Should Christians divorce and remarry? · spec 1.2.6

What this is about

Same Jesus, two texts — the whole divorce debate is about which verse you weight. Matthew 19:6: 'what God has joined together, let no one separate' grounds the Catholic position — marriage is a sacrament only death dissolves; civil divorce may protect a spouse, but remarriage is impossible without annulment. Matthew 19:9 — divorce allowed 'except for sexual immorality' — grounds the Protestant position: divorce is wrong but permitted when a marriage has truly died, because Christianity teaches forgiveness and fresh starts.

The secular view treats marriage as a legal institution with a right of exit, provided spouses part fairly and children are provided for. Argue it situationally, as many Christians in practice do: weigh peace and fresh starts against cost, hurt and lost contact. The Grade 9 point is precisely that neither Christian side ignores the Bible — they weight it differently; show that, and the 'Christians just disagree' answer becomes analysis.

Lesson 2

Gender prejudice and discrimination

Big question Should Christians work against gender discrimination? · spec 1.2.8

What this is about

This topic is a live campaign, not an abstraction: one Church changed, another holds the line. The Church of England, Methodists and Baptists ordain women as ministers and bishops, arguing from Genesis 1:27 and Galatians 3:28 — 'neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ'. The Catholic Church teaches equality and condemns gender prejudice, yet ordains only men, because the priest stands in the place of Christ. Some conservative churches go further: women should not teach or lead at all, citing the New Testament order as God-ordained, not discriminatory.

Secular critics would make refusing to ordain women illegal — equality law should not stop at the church door — and surveys show prejudice persists in workplaces everywhere. The strongest (d) distinguishes prejudice (attitude), discrimination (action) and doctrine (belief about office), then asks the sharp question: can a Church consistently preach Galatians 3:28 and reserve its highest office to men? Weigh the theology of representation against the theology of equality.

Paper 1 · Christianity

Paper 1 revision — Christian Beliefs · Living the Christian Life

No new content. Paper 1's beliefs units are rebuilt this week — key words, quotes, and the connections between beliefs that (c) questions probe.

Focus 1

Christian Beliefs

What this is about

Paper 1 Question 1 is Christian Beliefs — the load-bearing wall of the whole paper. The Trinity as the Nicene Creed defines it; creation and what Genesis 1 is FOR, not just what it says; the incarnation — God become human; the last days, resurrection and ascension; and salvation — how sin is dealt with and why the cross is the hinge. Revise these as an architecture, not a list: each belief supports the next, and (c) questions love to ask for the connections.

The unit ends on the problem of evil — the strongest attack the paper lets you meet: if God is omnipotent and benevolent, why evil and suffering? Learn it as clashing propositions, then the responses — free will, soul-making, prayer and charity, the example of Jesus — and be ready to say which response carries most weight and why. Fix the key words and the quotes this week: every mark in this unit assumes them.

Focus 2

Living the Christian Life

What this is about

Paper 1 Question 3 is Living the Christian Life — doctrine made visible. Liturgical against non-liturgical worship and WHY churches differ, not just that they do; the sacraments, especially baptism and eucharist, with the divergent views on what a sacrament actually does; prayer — set forms, informal prayer, and what the Lord's Prayer teaches about both. The examiner rewards the candidate who can explain the REASONING behind each practice from a named tradition.

Then the outward life: pilgrimage and the divergence over whether it is needful; Christmas and Easter as theology in festival form; mission, evangelism and church growth; and the Church in action — street pastors, food banks, Christian Aid and Tearfund. Named, specific examples are the currency here. Collect one per topic, and rehearse deploying each inside a (d) evaluation, not just a (b) description.

Paper 2 revision — Muslim Beliefs · Living the Muslim Life

Same discipline, other paper: Muslim Beliefs and Living the Muslim Life. Precision on Sunni and Shi'a differences is where grades separate.

Focus 1

Muslim Beliefs

What this is about

Paper 2 Question 1 is Muslim Beliefs — and precision separates the grades. The six Beliefs of Sunni Islam against the five Roots of Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam: know which is which and never blur them. Tawhid and the nature of Allah — the 99 names as evidence of character; Risalah, prophethood from Adam to Muhammad; the holy books and why the Qur'an stands above the earlier, corrupted revelations; Malaikah, the angels who record and reveal.

Finish with al-Qadr — predestination — and Akhirah, judgement and the afterlife, where belief becomes motive: every deed weighed, so every deed matters. The Sunni/Shi'a divergences are not trivia; they are exactly what (b) and (c) questions probe. This week is for locking the key words and the quotes so that next week's paper-writing is retrieval, not archaeology.

Focus 2

Living the Muslim Life

What this is about

Paper 2 Question 3 is Living the Muslim Life — the Five Pillars and beyond, each practice carrying its reasoning. Shahadah as the foundation the rest stand on; Salah five times daily and the divergences over how and when; Sawm in Ramadan and who is excused, and why the exemptions themselves teach mercy; Zakah as purification of wealth, with the Shi'a khums alongside; Hajj and what the rites re-enact.

Add the Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi'a Islam, jihad in both senses — the greater struggle within, the lesser without — and the festivals: Id-ul-Adha, Id-ul-Fitr, and Ashura with its different meaning for Shi'a Muslims. The trap in this unit is vagueness: 'Muslims pray' earns nothing; 'Salah performed at set times, facing the qiblah, because the Qur'an commands it' earns everything. Precision this week, marks next week.

Crime and Punishment · then the (d) clinic

Final week before the mock. The five steps of the 12-mark (d) get drilled until the structure is reflex, not effort.

Focus 1

Crime and Punishment — the Year 10 half

What this is about

Half of the Crime and Punishment content was taught in Year 10 — the mock assumes it, so this week reclaims it. Justice as the base concept: why it matters to society, and why Surah after Surah makes it God's own demand. Crime and its causes — poverty, upbringing, addiction, greed — and the Muslim insistence that explanation is not excuse. Good, evil and suffering: why people do evil, and how belief in judgement reframes the question.

Then attitudes to punishment, where the divergent views sharpen: severity as deterrence against reform as purpose, with the aims of punishment vocabulary — retribution, deterrence, reformation, protection — doing the analytical work. Retrieve it with the episodes and the key-word drill, because in the mock this material must surface on demand, faster than you re-learned it.

Focus 2

The (d) clinic — the 12-mark answer

What this is about

The 12-mark (d) is a third of each question, and it is won by structure, not inspiration. The five steps, drilled until they are reflex: state the divergent views precisely; develop the strongest case FOR with source and reasoning; steelman the case AGAINST — at its strongest, including the genuine non-religious voice where the question invites it; weigh both sides explicitly, strength against limit; conclude with a judgement that says WHY the winner survives the strongest counter.

SPaG rides on Q1(d) and Q3(d), so accuracy is marks, not manners. This week every practice answer ends with a two-sided weigh and a justified verdict — paragraphs that end with a LINK back to the question, a WEIGH closing each evaluation. By Friday the shape should be automatic, because in the mock you will have about thirteen minutes to build it.

Matters of Life and Death

New topics resume while the mocks are marked. The standard does not pause for results.

Lesson 1

Sanctity of life

Big question If life is sacred, may a Christian ever take a human life? · spec 1.4.2

What this is about

Sanctity of life — life as sacred because God creates it, breathes it and owns it — is not the easy answer; it is the reason the hard questions exist. Genesis 1:27 grounds it (made in God's image), Genesis 2:7 dramatises it (God breathes life into the first human). If life is truly sacred, may a Christian ever take it? Pacifists — Quakers, Pax Christi — say never. The Catechism disagrees: legitimate defence 'can be not only a right but a grave duty'.

The same principle is claimed by BOTH sides of the capital punishment debate: abolitionists argue a sacred life is never the state's to take; retentionists reply that the Bible itself prescribes death for murder precisely because the victim's life was sacred. That is the analytical prize of this topic — one principle, opposite conclusions — and a Grade 9 answer shows exactly where the reasoning forks.

Lesson 2

The issue of abortion

Big question What are Christian and non-religious attitudes to abortion? · spec 1.4.4

What this is about

UK law permits abortion under conditions; the ethical argument is over where the line falls. The Catholic position: life begins at conception, so abortion breaks 'You shall not murder' — the only exception the doctrine of double effect, where saving the mother unintentionally costs the foetus. Liberal Protestants argue Jesus' command to love your neighbour can make abortion the most loving option after rape or fatal diagnosis. Pro-choice arguments start elsewhere: a foetus is not a separate life until viability, so the woman's rights and quality of life come first.

Notice the debates are not symmetrical: the religious argument is about the STATUS of the foetus, the non-religious one about the RIGHTS of the woman — so the sides often talk past each other. Lesson 1

Non-religious arguments against life after death

Big question Is there reliable evidence for life after death? · spec 1.4.6

What this is about

The non-religious case against the afterlife has two prongs. First, the evidence fails: near-death experiences rest on fallible memories, mediums have been exposed as fraudulent — Thouless's coded message was never decoded — and the religions contradict each other about what survives. Second, and stronger: there is no ROOM for it — science finds no non-material realm, and Marx adds a motive, calling the belief 'pie in the sky', social control that keeps the poor content.

The Christian rebuttal must meet both prongs: the resurrection record and the disciples' willingness to die for it answer the evidence charge; Descartes' argument that mind is separate from body answers the no-room charge. Both sides actually agree on the hinge — everything turns on whether the evidence can be trusted and whether a soul could exist without a body. Argue THAT hinge, not the slogans, and weigh which side carries the burden of proof.

Euthanasia

What this is about

Lesson 1

Forgiveness and reconciliation

Big question No peace without justice — or no peace without forgiveness? · spec 2.4.2a

What this is about

No peace without justice — or no peace without forgiveness? Islam insists on both ends of the chain. Justice first: where laws are unjust people rebel, as Syria showed, so peace built on injustice cannot last. But God is 'the Merciful, the Compassionate' at the head of every surah but one, and Muslims who want God's forgiveness on the Day of Judgement must extend their own: Surah 49:9–10 commands believers to 'make peace between your brothers'.

The Grade 9 formulation: justice without mercy is cold, mercy without justice is blind — genuine peacemaking removes the CAUSE of conflict and heals the RELATIONSHIP. Use South Africa's truth-and-reconciliation model or the Qur'anic 'repel evil with what is better' as your worked example, then evaluate the hard case: when justice and forgiveness pull in opposite directions, which should a peacemaker sacrifice first?

Lesson 2

Just War theory

Big question Can a war ever be just — and must a Muslim fight it? · spec 2.4.5

What this is about

Just War theory is a checklist, and it works like one: just cause, proper authority, last resort, proportional methods, civilians protected — a war passes only if it ticks EVERY box; one miss and it is not just. Muslim teaching adds force: if a war genuinely meets the conditions, fighting in it is a duty, because Surah 4:75 demands the defence of the oppressed — 'why should ye not fight in the cause of those who, being weak, are ill-treated?'

Lesson 1

Holy War and jihad

Big question When, if ever, is a war holy? · spec 2.4.6

What this is about

Distinguish or die: every Holy War is jihad, but almost no jihad is Holy War. The greater jihad — the inner struggle to be a good Muslim — involves no fighting at all; Holy War (Harb al-Muqadis) is only ever the lesser jihad, permitted to defend Islam, protect Muslims' freedom of worship, or right a wrong. Even then modern Islamic lawyers confine it: last resort, proper authority, prospect of success, minimum suffering — and the hadith forbid killing 'any child, any woman, or any elder or sick person'.

Handle Surah 9:5 ('fight and slay the pagans wherever you find them') like a scholar: read it in context, against the restrictions that surround it — that is the difference between analysis and quotation. Atheists and Humanists reject the whole category: no God, so no war can be holy. Weigh whether the concept, so hedged that it barely applies, still does moral work — or whether 'holy war' is a contradiction in terms.

Lesson 2

Weapons of mass destruction

Big question Should countries possess weapons of mass destruction? · spec 2.4.7

What this is about

Weapons of mass destruction put the Just War theory under its heaviest load. By its own rules a good Muslim cannot USE them — they deliberately target the civilians Islam forbids harming, and Ayatollah Khamenei condemned them outright — yet Pakistan keeps nuclear weapons, which shows the possession/use distinction doing real work. Jewish law runs parallel: Lord Jakobovits argued a defensive nuclear ATTACK 'can never be justified', while threatening one might be.

The strongest secular defence is mutually assured destruction: because both sides would die, no nuclear power attacks, and there has been no world war since 1945. Steelman it properly, then push: peace that lasts only until someone miscalculates is not peace but postponement — and the money is a moral fact too. Weigh deterrence against Surah 5:32's arithmetic that to kill one innocent is 'as if he slew the whole people'.

Lesson 1

Marriage

Big question How important is marriage — and is cohabitation acceptable? · spec 1.2.1

What this is about

For Christians marriage is not a social contract but part of creation's design: 'at the beginning of creation God made them male and female… and the two will become one flesh' (Mark 10:6–8). Each link follows from the last — design, union, God's bond, so 'what God has joined together, let no one separate'. That chain is why Catholic teaching treats marriage as the only right place for sex, and why cohabitation falls short of what marriage IS, not just what it looks like.

Liberal Protestants accept cohabitation as 'a step along the way', expecting marriage when children come; secular views treat the whole question as private, while still mostly valuing marriage for stability and public commitment. In the exam, argue the chain: if you grant the creation design, the strict conclusion follows; the real divergence is over whether Genesis describes every couple or an ideal. Weigh what is lost — and what is honestly admitted — when marriage becomes optional.

Lesson 2

Sexual relationships

Big question What are the different attitudes to homosexuality? · spec 1.2.2

What this is about

Christian sexual ethics rests on one premise: 'your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit… you are not your own' (1 Corinthians 6:19). Sexual sin is uniquely 'against your own body' because the body is sacred — that is the logic under every rule. Lesson 1

Support for the family in the local parish

Big question Do families need the support of the local parish? · spec 1.2.4

What this is about

Does a family need the parish? Catholic logic runs: the parish baptised the child, so it owes the family help in keeping the baptism promises — church schools, First Communion classes, youth work; the family in turn is the nursery of the faith, which is why the system is not optional but survival. Jesus' welcome — 'let the little children come to me' (Matthew 19:14) — anchors it. Non-parish churches (Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal) run family worship, Messy Church and counselling: no parish STRUCTURE, same SUPPORT.

The non-religious answer: a family can raise children safely and morally with no church at all — schools, clubs and grandparents do the same jobs. Meet it honestly: what the parish adds is not childcare but formation in a community of belief, plus support at the hinges of life (birth, marriage, death). Evaluate whether that is a service families need, or one the Church needs families to want.

Lesson 2

Equality of men and women in the family

Big question Should men and women have equal roles in the family? · spec 1.2.7

Lesson 1

The aims of punishment

Big question What are the four aims of punishment — and which matters most? · spec 2.2.5

What this is about

Four aims, one act of punishing: protection keeps the law-abiding safe from the violent; retribution makes criminals pay in proportion to the harm — Muslims allow the victim's family compensation as its measure; deterrence makes crime too costly to dare — the Muslim defence of severe hudud penalties is exactly that amputation deters where six months inside does not; reformation teaches the offender to live differently, and Islamic practice lets punished offenders remain in society rather than rot outside it.

The analytical key: retribution looks BACK at the crime; the other three look FORWARD to prevent the next one — and Islam wants both, since Surah 4:26 speaks of God making the ordinances clear. Genesis 9:6 ('whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed') shows scripture grounding retribution in the image of God itself. Evaluate by asking which aim should win when they conflict — deterrence demands severity, reformation mercy — and defend your ordering.

Lesson 1

The treatment of criminals

Big question How should criminals be treated — and can torture ever be justified? · spec 2.2.7

What this is about

Rights for criminals are not softness — they are the only shield an innocent suspect has. The mainstream position across Islam, Judaism and secular human-rights law converges: fair trial, legal representation, humane treatment, no torture. The Cairo Declaration forbids torture; the hadith warns 'Allah will torture those who torture people'; Surah 76:8 praises feeding 'the indigent, the orphan, and the captive'. The UN Declaration says the same without God.

The exam wants the pressure points, not the consensus: the ticking-bomb argument for torture in extremis, and Judaism's rodef (pursuer) principle, which some stretch toward preventive harshness. Steelman the ticking bomb, then dismantle it — unreliable information, corrupted institutions, and the certainty that torturing the guilty will sometimes mean torturing the innocent. Weigh whether a right that bends under pressure was ever a right at all.

Lesson 2

The death penalty

Big question Can capital punishment be justified? · spec 2.2.8

What this is about

Can capital punishment be justified? Islam's sources say yes, within law: Surah 5:36 prescribes it for waging war against God, Surah 6:151 forbids taking life 'except by way of justice and the law', and Muhammad sentenced murderers — yet some Muslims note the Qur'an ALLOWS death without REQUIRING it, and prefer mercy. The Humanist case (IHEU 1989) attacks on three fronts: the right to life is absolute; no court is infallible, so the innocent are executed; and the statistics show no deterrent effect. That last point is structural: if deterrence fails, the strongest retentionist argument collapses into pure retribution. A Grade 9 conclusion names its decisive criterion — irreversibility against justice — and defends it against the strongest counter, not the weakest.
